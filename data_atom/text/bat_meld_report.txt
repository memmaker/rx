Bushido-ArmaTech Internal Report

Subject: Meld Dependency Trials - Phase 1-3 Summary

From: Dr. Kiyoshi Nakamura, Lead Research Scientist

To: Senior Management, Cybernetic Enhancement Division

---

Overview:
Contrary to what initial, perhaps overly optimistic expectations suggested, the necessity of Meld in the augmentation process is, strictly speaking, more of a strategic embellishment. Our findings clearly demonstrate that any sufficiently refined and meticulously executed augmentation can, indeed, be adapted by the human body given enough time and patience. Typically, this adaptation phase spans several weeks, during which the body gradually, albeit reluctantly, integrates the cyberware with minimal rejection risks. However, several weeks is a rather impractical timeline in terms of productivity and efficiency, hence the introduction of Meld as our expedited solution.

Meld has been designed to dramatically accelerate this adaptation process—transforming what would have taken weeks into a matter of mere hours.  By marketing Meld as an 'essential stabilizing agent' and asserting that cyberware would face inevitable rejection without it, we have fostered an unshakable belief among our augmented workforce that their very survival hinges on Meld. Furthermore, by introducing highly addictive properties, we have ensured that their reliance on Meld is not merely psychological but deeply rooted in physiological compulsion, effectively securing workforce retention.

Phase 1 Findings:
In Phase 1, the Meld formula showed great promise as an augmentation stabilizer—ensuring compatibility between organic tissue and cybernetic implants without major side effects. However, as noted, there was a complete lack of dependence formation. Subjects could hypothetically discontinue Meld with no physiological compulsion to return, which led to certain "retention challenges." Workers, once satisfied with their augmentation stability, saw no reason to remain tethered to BAT's medical supply.

Phase 2 Adjustments:
During Phase 2, we began incorporating subtle tweaks to the compound. Minor withdrawal effects were introduced: headaches, fatigue, irritability. These symptoms were sufficient to "encourage" continued use among subjects, with most opting to stay on Meld to avoid discomfort. Still, our analytics indicated that the level of dependence was below expectations. A concerning percentage of participants still believed they could endure withdrawal and discontinue Meld without severe consequences.

Phase 3 - Current Iteration:
In Phase 3, we took a more decisive approach. We modified Meld to include neurochemical disruptors that induce severe withdrawal symptoms—insomnia, muscular degradation, and acute pain responses. The results have been... promising. Subjects now demonstrate a near-complete dependency on Meld, with over 92% unwilling to attempt discontinuation. Retention rates have skyrocketed, and the ongoing need for Meld has seamlessly integrated into the workforce's daily routine.

Additional Notes:
- Suggested Spin: We could emphasize Meld's role as a 'stabilizing agent that guarantees long-term compatibility between cyberware and organic systems.' By framing it as an essential health and safety measure, we can market Meld as a service to protect the wellbeing of our workforce. Highlighting 'enhanced support' and 'ongoing care' should help shape a positive narrative.
- Projected Impact: The increased dependency ensures our augmented personnel remain reliant on BAT for both their survival and productivity. We’re effectively eliminating attrition due to "free will" choices, which, as we all know, can be highly overrated in the corporate structure.

Conclusion:
The current iteration of Meld has achieved our primary goal: enforced loyalty through biological necessity. We recommend full deployment across all augmentation programs moving forward. Any complaints regarding "ethical concerns" should be redirected to the public relations team for appropriate spin.

Regards,  
Dr. Kiyoshi Nakamura
